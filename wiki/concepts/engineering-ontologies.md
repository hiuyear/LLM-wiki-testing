# Engineering Ontologies

**What it is**: A formal, machine-readable representation of concepts and relationships within an engineering domain. An ontology defines *what exists* (classes of entities) and *how they relate* (properties, constraints, hierarchies). Unlike a database schema (which organizes data), an ontology organizes *knowledge* — enabling reasoning, inference, and reuse across different systems and campaigns.

In chemical engineering: an ontology might define "Reactor," "Compartment," "Interface," "MassTransfer," "Reaction," and the allowed relationships between them. A workflow can then query the ontology to ask: "what building blocks are valid for a CSTR?" or "what phenomena should be expected in a two-phase system?"

---

## Why ontologies matter for autonomous agents

An autonomous agent that selects tools, generates models, or plans experiments needs a shared vocabulary and a set of rules for what's valid. Ontologies provide this:
- **Constraint propagation**: rules in the ontology eliminate invalid actions before the agent tries them
- **Knowledge reuse**: artifacts from one campaign (e.g., a validated model topology) can be retrieved and used in the next
- **Expert knowledge injection**: domain experts encode their knowledge once; the agent uses it repeatedly without re-asking

The alternative — hardcoding these rules in the agent's logic — is brittle and doesn't generalize across systems.

## OntoCAPE

OntoCAPE is the dominant process systems engineering ontology. It covers:
- Chemical process units and their interconnections
- Physicochemical phenomena (reactions, mass transfer, heat transfer)
- Mathematical models (equations, parameters, variables)
- Material properties

Laub et al. (2026) extend OntoCAPE with a "modeling subdomain" that stores building blocks, graph grammar rules, reward shaping functions, and model topologies generated by their RL workflow. This allows the workflow to accumulate and reuse knowledge across modeling campaigns.

---

## Structure of the Laub et al. ontology

Two subdomains:

**Process subdomain** (physical reality):
- Reactor conditions, operating states
- Known phenomena (reactions, mass transfer)
- Material properties

**Modeling subdomain** (virtual realm):
- Mathematical objects (variables, parameters, equations in postfix notation)
- Building blocks (CSTR, PFR, FILM interface, mixing unit) with associated equation scaffolds
- Graph grammar rules (which topological manipulations are valid)
- Reward shaping rules (SWRL reasoning rules that assign R₁ based on topology structure)
- Candidate model topologies and their performance history

The two subdomains are connected: a "FILM interface" building block in the modeling subdomain maps to "diffusive mass transfer" in the process subdomain. This linkage enables automated inference — if the process ontology says "this reaction requires diffusive mass transfer," the modeling ontology can infer that a FILM interface building block is required.

## Ontology vs. knowledge graph

These terms are often conflated:
- **Knowledge graph**: stores *facts* (entities and their relationships) — often populated from data
- **Ontology**: stores *schema* (the vocabulary, rules, and constraints) — designed by experts

In practice: an ontology defines the types; a knowledge graph instantiates them with specific entities. Laub et al. use an ontology to manage the *types* of building blocks and grammar rules, and the knowledge graph to store specific *instances* (e.g., "the model generated on 2025-04-10 for TCR at 360 rpm has topology X and RMSD Y").

## Relevance to El Agente Gráfico

El Agente Gráfico needs some form of structured knowledge management:
- What graphical operations are valid on a given diagram type?
- What entities does a P&ID contain, and what relationships connect them?
- How should tool-use actions be constrained to prevent nonsensical combinations?

An ontology (or at minimum, a schema-driven knowledge graph) answers these questions and decouples them from the agent's core logic. The DEXPI standard ([[entities/dexpi-standard]]) is itself an ontology for P&ID components — it defines what a valve, sensor, or pipe is in machine-readable terms.
